Orders: making iteration cheap

A multi-screen product area where every piece of feedback used to cost a sweep across screens. I moved it into a high-fidelity model built on reusable local components, so the unit of change became the pattern instead of the screen.

  • RoleDesign manager, and individual contributor
  • VerticalTrading
  • Built onThe FYERS design system, plus local components
  • The shiftThe unit of iteration moved from screen to pattern
01

The problem

Orders is a core product experience at FYERS. Traders need to understand, monitor and manage orders across many states and scenarios. It spans multiple screens with heavily repeated UI patterns.

Even with a shared design system underneath, the project workflow still ran low fidelity, review, high-fidelity UI, more review. For a connected, multi-screen experience, that made one piece of feedback expensive: find every affected screen, update each one, review the lot, and repeat the next time a shared pattern moved.

The challenge was not creating the screens. It was reducing the effort of iterating across a connected product.

02

The hypothesis

I was not trying to skip exploration or jump straight to visual design. I wanted to test something narrower: if repeated patterns were structured as reusable local components, could I work at higher fidelity and make iteration cheaper?

03

What I did

I moved directly into a high-fidelity working model and treated no screen as an isolated design. I built reusable local components inside the project and assembled the connected experience from them.

A change to a shared pattern became one edit to a component instead of a sweep across screens.

I wasn’t trying to skip wireframing. I was trying to reduce the cost of iteration.

04

What it changed

Working in high fidelity earlier narrowed a gap I had lived with for years: the distance between what we agreed at wireframe stage and what the UI eventually became. Stakeholders reviewed decisions in something close to the real product, so their feedback was about the real thing.

  • Faster iteration, with less repetitive screen-level work
  • Stronger consistency across the connected experience
  • Feedback that was easier to absorb, because it landed on patterns
05

What I took from it

I had treated fidelity as a way to control how much detail a conversation carried. Orders showed me it is also an operational decision: a well-structured high-fidelity model can be the cheaper place to iterate.

That is not a rule for everything. The right fidelity depends on the problem, the maturity of the system underneath, how much UI repeats, and whether stakeholders need to judge the final experience to give useful feedback. For Orders, it was the right call.
