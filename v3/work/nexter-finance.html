Nexter Finance: shipping a market while the scope kept moving

A decentralised prediction market for crypto, stocks, commodities and real-world events. I led its design from the first flow, and changed the plan so the team could launch long before the full product was defined.

  • 10K+Users

Nexter’s figures, as published in my case study in January 2024.

The problem
Two products, five markets and six networks to launch, with an incomplete brief, a moving scope and little time for design.
My role
Design Lead from zero on desktop and mobile web. I designed the product myself and managed a visual designer, a motion designer and an intern.
The call
Launch the simpler product first and release it market by market, instead of waiting for a complete scope.
The result
A live product that went on to 1.7M+ predictions and $19M+ in volume by January 2024.
01

The situation

Nexter, formerly Bhavish Finance, set out to launch a decentralised prediction market in two forms. In Lossy predictions, people commit crypto on whether an asset will go up or down within a round, and the winners are paid from the pool. In No-loss predictions, they deposit stablecoins and predict with game tokens that earn yield, so the deposit itself is never at stake. No-loss was the headline: the landing page promised people could predict the future without losing money.

The first launch was meant to cover crypto, stocks, commodities, automated prediction vaults and real-world event quests, on six blockchain networks. The brief was incomplete, the scope kept moving, and the time set aside for design was short.

The research gave the product its bar. There was real demand, especially for crypto predictions among people who already knew how prediction markets work. But the category had obvious weaknesses: markets are zero-sum for participants, liquidity is thin, and most products gave people little reason to come back.

02

My role

I set the design direction from the first flow and designed the desktop and mobile web product myself. I managed a team of three, a visual designer, a motion designer and an intern I trained, inside a fully remote, cross-functional team.

Design also owned motion, the marketing campaigns and the landing page. As engineering built each release, I reviewed every screen against the design and logged the gaps for the developers, so what shipped matched what was agreed.

03

Shipping while the scope moved

No-loss, the headline product, was stuck: its requirements weren’t ready, and the flow diagram and architecture couldn’t be drawn without them. Waiting would have meant launching nothing.

The call

Launch Lossy predictions first, and hold No-loss until its requirements were ready.

Why
Lossy could ship. It would put a real product in people’s hands, test whether Lossy had a market, and give leadership time to get No-loss right.
The trade-off
The product the landing page promised would arrive last, and Lossy had to carry the launch on its own.

Leadership agreed. Then I argued against launching everything in Lossy at once.

The call

Release Lossy market by market: crypto, stocks and commodities first, then vaults, then quests, with No-loss last.

Why
Each market could be finished, every use case covered, before the next began. The development team aligned with it.
The trade-off
People would see a narrower product at launch, and wait longer for the rest.

Waiting for a settled scope would have meant not shipping.

04

Designing for two kinds of user

The people most likely to use Nexter already knew web3, so the main screen stays out of their way. The round, the asset and the time left are visible at a glance, and a prediction is two decisions: up or down, and how much.

A wallet, though, is the first wall a newcomer hits. Alongside the usual wallet connection, people without a web3 wallet can sign in with email or a social account, through MagicLink, and first-time visitors get coach marks on the key actions. The product serves the experts without shutting out everyone else.

05

Adding No-loss without breaking what worked

By the time No-loss was ready, Nexter was integrated with Quickswap and a lot of people were using it through both Quickswap and the Nexter app. Leadership asked me not to change the interface they had learned.

The call

Add No-loss behind the currency selector, as Nexter’s own token, rather than redesign the app around it.

Why
The people already trading had learned the Lossy interface, and many arrived through a partner’s app. Disrupting them risked the traction the launch had won.
The trade-off
No-loss became harder to find than the headline product deserved. A guided walkthrough explains it the first time, and deposits, withdrawals, reinvesting and claiming rewards all live in the portfolio.
06

Testing with six people

With both products designed, I ran in-house usability testing with six people: three who knew prediction platforms and three who had never used one.

  • People who knew the category finished tasks much faster. Newcomers couldn’t finish unaided, but could with guidance.
  • Both groups struggled to understand how No-loss worked.
  • Both wanted the key facts on every market card, so they could compare options and decide.

The second finding is the one I would have acted on first. I moved on from Nexter soon after this round, so the findings were left to the team.

07

Outcome

Nexter launched, then grew release by release. After launch I watched Google Analytics and Hotjar to see how people really used the app. Partnerships with decentralised exchanges such as Quickswap, Zeroswap, Nautilus Chain and Heliswap, leaderboard campaigns and the community brought people in.

By January 2024, people had made more than 1.7 million predictions, worth over $19 million, on a product that had started without a settled scope.

08

What I’d do differently

  • Test with real users before development, not after. The timeline didn’t allow it. Earlier testing is exactly what would have surfaced the No-loss confusion while it was still cheap to fix.
  • Agree a minimum scope before agreeing dates. The phased release protected the launch, but a scope that kept changing still moved the design timeline more than once.
