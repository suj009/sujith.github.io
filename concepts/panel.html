The panel asks

How do you define the standard for craft — and how do you hold it when there's pressure?

Sujith

The clearest example is the Options Scalper Terminal. Most fintech design makes the complex feel simple. This was the opposite: the user wants complexity, at speed, with zero margin for error. High-frequency options traders live in seconds — a slow interaction or an ambiguous state isn't an annoyance, it's money.

I set the direction and defended it: a fixed layout that becomes muscle memory, deeper analytics behind progressive disclosure so the default view stays scannable, live state read through colour and motion rather than full redraws, and error-prevention at speed — a deliberate confirm step and guardrails against fat-finger mistakes.

The standard I held against pressure to show everything: the terminal should disappear into muscle memory. That's a design standard you can argue for in a room full of stakeholders, because it's about the trader's hands, not my taste.

What I actually did Design lead and oversight for the options vertical, ~3 months in Q2 2024. I owned the interaction direction and the call on what stayed out of the default view — not the visual production alone.

The panel asks

Tell me about a time you changed how a design organisation works.

Sujith

When I joined FYERS, design didn't have one language — it had several dialects. Style guides were scattered, half-finished, or duplicated, so the same element existed in different ways across web, iOS, and Android. That cost the business twice: designers rebuilt instead of designed, and users met a product that felt subtly inconsistent — costly in a product built on trust.

I argued for a single, versioned source of truth rather than more scattered guides. After v1.0 I took over and drove FDSG 2.0 and 2.1 across web and mobile — a visual reset from gradient-heavy to clean and minimal, then a pass that tightened spacing and density.

But the system wasn't the real change — the org was. I helped move FYERS from a split UX/UI relay to end-to-end Product Designers. Adoption was fast because the people using the system understood it top to bottom. And when engineering built it into code, we were honest that design-to-code was never a 100% match — naming the gap is what kept the system improving instead of quietly rotting.

What I actually did Design Review Board member under v1.0, then owner of v2.0 and v2.1 across web and mobile with an 8-person team. I drove the org change alongside the system, not as a side effect of it.

The panel asks

How do you grow designers — and what's it like to work for you?

Sujith

I optimize for clarity and ownership. I'd rather a designer own a problem end to end — from user need to shipped screen — than pass it down a relay, because handoffs are where intent dies. That's the same conviction that drove the UX/UI change, applied to individual careers instead of process diagrams.

I try to lead the way I wish I'd been led: a clear direction, room to do the best work of your career, and systems that make the next project easier. I push for craft, I'll get into the code when it helps, and I care more about the team getting sharper than about being the smartest person in the room.

Before design was my job I was the person running the interviews and diary studies as an HCI researcher at Microsoft and IBM — which is mostly why I'd rather ask a user than win an argument.
