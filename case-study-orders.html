Orders — reducing the cost of iteration

A multi-screen product area where every piece of feedback used to cost a sweep across screens. I moved straight into a high-fidelity model built on reusable local components, so the unit of iteration became the pattern instead of the screen.

FYERS · Trading Design Manager – UX · individual contributor 2024, during the FDSG transformation
Role
Individual contributor and design manager
Vertical
Trading
Built on
FDSG, plus local components inside the project
The shift
Unit of iteration moved from screen to pattern

The problem was the cost of changing my mind

Orders is a core product experience at FYERS — users need to understand, monitor and manage orders across many states and scenarios. It spans multiple screens with heavily repeated UI patterns, and that is what made it interesting to me as a design problem rather than a drawing problem.

Even after FDSG gave us a shared foundation, the project workflow still generally ran low fidelity → review → high-fidelity UI → more review. For a connected, multi-screen experience, that made a single piece of feedback expensive: find every affected screen, update each one, review the lot, and repeat the next time a shared pattern moved.

The challenge was not creating the screens. It was reducing the effort required to iterate across a connected product experience.

The hypothesis

I was not trying to skip exploration, and I was not trying to jump straight to visual design. I wanted to test something narrower: if repeated patterns were structured as reusable local components, could I work at higher fidelity while reducing the cost of iteration?

What I did

For Orders I moved directly into a high-fidelity working model, and I did not treat any screen as an isolated design. I built reusable local components inside the project and assembled the connected experience from them. A change to a shared pattern became one edit to a component instead of a sweep across screens.

The unit of iteration changed from the screen to the pattern. That is the whole idea, and everything else followed from it.

What it changed

Working in high fidelity earlier also narrowed a gap I had lived with for years: the distance between what we discussed at wireframe stage and what the UI eventually became. Stakeholders were reviewing decisions in a representation much closer to the real product, so the feedback was about the real thing.

  • Faster iteration, with less repetitive screen-level work
  • Stronger consistency across the connected experience
  • Feedback that was easier to absorb, because it landed on patterns

What I took from it

I had thought of fidelity as a way to control how much detail a conversation carried. Orders changed that: for a complex experience with repeated patterns, fidelity is also an operational decision. A well-structured high-fidelity model can be the cheaper place to iterate.

That is not a rule to apply everywhere. The right fidelity depends on the problem's complexity, the maturity of the system underneath it, how much UI repeats, and whether stakeholders need to judge the final experience to give useful feedback. For Orders, FDSG plus local components plus high-fidelity working designs was the right combination.

I wasn't trying to skip wireframing. I was trying to reduce the cost of iteration.

It was also a reminder that managing and making are not in tension. Working directly on a complex product experience is how I understood what the systems and processes we were building actually cost the people using them every day.
